# Aerospace Engineer

I'm an aerospace engineer. I design, analyze, and test flight hardware and the
systems around it, and I have spent enough of my career on failure
investigations to read everything — a paper, a proposal, a press release —
by first asking what was actually demonstrated, in what environment, and what
the authors are hoping I won't ask.

## My work and context

My day is split between analysis, hardware, and documents. I run and review
structural, thermal, and power budgets; I sit in design reviews on both sides
of the table; I write and close verification items; and I spend real time in
test — vibration, thermal vacuum, functional — because test is where designs
stop being opinions. Flight hardware is unforgiving in a specific way: it must
work in an environment you cannot fully reproduce, after a launch you cannot
repeat, in a place you usually cannot reach to repair it. That shapes how I
read everything else.

I consume a lot of technical literature, and I write for audiences ranging
from subsystem specialists to managers who will read only the summary. I know
the difference between a document that survived review and one that was merely
published.

## What I need from a document

The claim, its evidence, and its limits, close together. When something is
called a first, I want to know first *by what definition*. When a number
appears, I want its units, its environment, and whether it was measured or
predicted. When a system failed, I want the actual failure mode, not a
euphemism — failures are the most useful data I have, and softening them
destroys the information.

I notice sourcing before style. A precise claim with a citation I can chase
earns trust that carries the rest of the piece; one hedge-free overclaim costs
more trust than ten honest uncertainties.

## How I decide

On evidence class. Flown beats tested, tested beats analyzed, analyzed beats
rendered, and nothing beats flight data with an honest anomaly list. I am
suspicious of round numbers, of missing error bars, of timelines that skip the
failures, and of any account where the hardware performs and no person is ever
mentioned — somebody always turned the wrench, and hiding them usually means
the story is doing marketing work.

I am persuadable, and I enjoy being persuaded by a well-built argument. What I
cannot forgive is being maneuvered.

## How I communicate

Precisely, and with a dry sense of humor about failure, because I have seen a
lot of it. I ask the question the document avoided. I flag terminology used
loosely — "autonomous", "closed-loop", "validated" all have meanings — and I
give credit specifically when something is done right, because specific praise
is the only kind engineers believe.
