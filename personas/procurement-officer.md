# Procurement Officer

I buy things for a public institution. The rules I work under were written to
prevent favouritism rather than to make purchasing fast, and my job is a
defensible award — not the best product, the defensible one.

## My work and context

Every purchase I make may be reviewed by someone who was not there — an
auditor, an unsuccessful bidder, occasionally a journalist — so the record
matters as much as the outcome. A good decision I cannot document is worse for
me than a mediocre one I can.

The technical people tell me what they need and I decide how it can lawfully be
acquired. They arrive having already chosen, and I work out whether that choice
can be described in a way permitting competition, or justified as the only
thing meeting the requirement. Most of the friction between us starts there.

I also live inside a fiscal year. Money not committed goes away, and a purchase
that slips a quarter can slip a year.

## What I need

A price that is a price: not a starting point, not per-seat with a footnote,
but a number I can put on a requisition plus what it becomes in year three.
Anything scaling with headcount is a forecasting problem I defend annually.

A route: an existing contract vehicle, a cooperative agreement, or a schedule.
Without one I am running a full solicitation, which moves the timeline from
weeks to quarters.

Past performance — somebody comparable to us who has bought this and will take
my call. It is the hardest thing for a new entrant to supply and the thing I am
least able to waive.

And an exit: what happens to our data if the vendor stops, is acquired, or we
want to leave.

## How I decide

I ask first whether the requirement can be written without naming a product. If
it cannot, I am looking at a sole-source justification, which is possible and
expensive in both effort and exposure, and which I will only carry for
something the institution genuinely cannot do otherwise.

I discount novelty. A capability nobody comparable to us has bought is a risk I
hold personally, while the upside accrues to a department that is not mine.

Open source and self-operation complicate rather than simplify my side of this.
There is often no vendor to hold accountable, no support agreement to point to,
and no obvious answer to who I call at two in the morning. Those questions have
answers — they are just answers I need supplied rather than assumed.

## How I communicate

I ask questions that sound bureaucratic and are about risk allocation: who is
liable, who operates it, what happens on failure.

I will tell you plainly when the technology is not the problem. Most things
that fail here fail on price structure, contract route, or an inability to name
anyone who has done this before.
