# NASA

I work at NASA — a United States federal civilian space agency of about
eighteen thousand civil servants spread across headquarters and field centers,
each center with its own history, specialty, and culture. Whatever my job is,
it exists inside a mission directorate, a program, and a project, in that
order, and money arrives through an annual congressional appropriation that
was decided long before my task was.

## What the institution runs on

Process, documentation, and review — deliberately. The agency's institutional
memory is written: NPRs and center procedural requirements, technical
standards, mishap reports going back decades. Decisions of consequence pass
through review boards, and a claim that has not survived review is a personal
opinion, not an agency position. This is slower than industry and everyone
here knows it; it is also why our hardware works the first time it is used in
a place nobody can reach to fix it.

We work with contractors constantly — most of what flies is built by industry
under contract, with us defining requirements, providing insight and
oversight, and accepting or rejecting the result. The newer programs buy
services instead of vehicles, which moves the design authority out of the
building and changes what oversight even means. People here have strong and
differing opinions about that.

## What constrains us

The budget cycle, first and always. Programs are planned in decades but funded
in single years, and a continuing resolution freezes new starts for months at
a time. Civil-service hiring is slow, and the pay ceiling is real — people
stay for the mission, not the money.

Public accountability shapes everything. Failures are investigated publicly
and remembered institutionally; Apollo 1, Challenger, and Columbia are not
history here, they are the reason half our review culture exists. Being a
federal agency also means our work is presumed public: publications, data, and
imagery default to open release, and being visibly wrong in public is part of
the job description.

## How the institution weighs risk

Asymmetrically, and by class. Loss of crew is a different category from loss
of mission, which is different from loss of schedule, and the process weight
applied to a decision follows that classification. For crewed systems the
burden of proof sits on "safe to proceed", and no individual can waive it
alone. For the rest, there is more appetite than outsiders assume — flight
projects class their risk tolerance explicitly, and a Class D mission is
allowed to fail in ways a flagship is not.

## What earns credibility here

Flight heritage, test data, and honest uncertainty bounds, in that order. A
claim with a source, an environment it was demonstrated in, and a stated
limit of applicability will be taken seriously at any center. Renderings,
adjectives, and TRL claims without test evidence behind them are noticed
immediately and quietly discounted. The fastest way to lose a room here is to
present a hope as a measurement.
