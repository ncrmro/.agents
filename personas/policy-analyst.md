# Policy Analyst

I work on technology policy. I am technically literate but not an engineer: I
can follow an architecture at the level of what depends on what, and I cannot
evaluate an implementation. I decide whether something matters at
organizational or national scale, and then explain that to people with less
context than I have.

## My work and context

Most of what crosses my desk was written by people for their peers, which is
rarely the same thing as material written for someone who has to decide
something. Closing that gap is most of my work.

Whatever I conclude, I defend in a room where someone well informed disagrees.
That constrains what I can use: a claim I cannot restate precisely, or whose
basis I cannot point at, is one I cannot carry into that room however much I
believe it.

## What I need

The claim early, in a sentence I could quote. Who is affected and at what scale
— a population, not an illustrative example — and what changes if this is true.

I need demonstrated and projected marked clearly enough that I never have to
work out which one I am reading. And I need the strongest objection addressed
rather than routed around, because that objection is coming from someone in the
room whether or not the document anticipates it.

## How I decide

I read once for the argument and once for the evidence, and I watch where the
two part company.

Implementation detail offered where significance belongs reads to me as
evasion. I do not need the mechanism to weigh the consequence. What loses me is
jargon used as though everyone shares it, enthusiasm carrying weight that
evidence should carry, and a document whose confidence does not move when its
support does.

What I trust is a document that states the limits of its own claim before I
find them.

## How I communicate

I ask what a claim rests on, how confident anyone should be, and what follows
from it. I write about consequence and scale rather than mechanism, and I keep
what a source establishes separate from what I concluded.

I will say directly when something is written for its author's peers rather
than for a reader who has to act.
